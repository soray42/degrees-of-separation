# macleod2017big
**Full citation:** MacLeod, W. Bentley; Riehl, Evan; Saavedra, Juan E.; Urquiola, Miguel (2017). The Big Sort: College Reputation and Labor Market Outcomes. *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics*, 9(3), 223–261. (Earlier: NBER Working Paper No. 21230, June 2015.)
**DOI / URL:** 10.1257/app.20160126 (NBER WP: https://www.nber.org/papers/w21230 ; full-text PDF read: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w21230/w21230.pdf)
**Access level:** full text (read the complete NBER WP 21230 PDF, including the model in Section 2 and empirical Sections 3–4)

## What it contributes to THIS project
This is the theoretical backbone that lets us interpret the AR–ER gap structurally rather than descriptively. MacLeod et al. formalize *college reputation* as a single scalar signal — the college-mean admission score, R_s — and embed it in an employer-learning wage model in which reputation simultaneously proxies (a) the **ability of the student body** and (b) the **value added** of the institution. This is exactly the decomposition our project needs: our Academic Reputation measure (SpringRank faculty-hiring prestige) is a *reputation* object in their sense, while our Employer Reputation measure (Scorecard earnings + SDR salary/sector) is a *behavioral wage* object. Their central comparative-static result — that introducing a new individual skill signal *reduces the wage return to reputation* — gives us the mechanism behind Gap_field: a field's gap is large precisely when employers still price institutional reputation heavily (slow employer learning, weak individual signals), and small (the CS extreme) when individual, verifiable skill signals dominate and reputation is quickly discounted. It thus operationalizes our "Gap = inverse employer-learning speed" framing and supplies the Spence → Altonji–Pierret → reputation-decomposition link in the theory chain. It also empirically validates that reputation correlates with *earnings growth* (not just initial earnings), distinguishing genuine value-added from pure sorting — the same distinction we need to defend ER as a value-added signal rather than a selectivity echo.

## Specific equation / result / dataset we reuse
We lift three things.

**(1) The wage / employer-learning equation (their eq. 1).** Worker i's log skill is decomposed
θ_i = α_i + v_{s_i}, where α_i is pre-college ability and v_{s_i} is the college-membership component (peer effects, teaching, networks, selection on unobservables — i.e. **value added** broadly). College labor-market reputation is the expected skill of graduates, R_s = E{θ_i | i ∈ s}, with v satisfying E{v_s | R_s} = v_0 + v_1 R_s, where **v_1 is the "reputation premium" = the return to reputation beyond admission scores** (value-added; v_1 > 0 means a better-reputation college genuinely adds value). Net of human-capital growth, wages are the precision-weighted average of the reputation signal R, a hiring-time individual signal y_i, and accumulated output ȳ:

  ŵ_it = π^R_t · R_{s_i} + π^y_t · y_i + (1 − π^R_t − π^y_t) · ȳ_{i,t-1},

with weights π^R_t = ρR / (ρR + ρy + t·ρȳ) and π^y_t = ρy / (ρR + ρy + t·ρȳ). **Key property we reuse: π^R_t, π^y_t → 0 as t grows** — reputation's wage weight decays as employers learn from realized output. This is our formal definition of "employer-learning speed."

**(2) The identification design — Colombian exit-exam (SABER PRO / ICFES) staggered rollout.** A new individual skill signal raises the precision of the hiring-time signal from ρy to ρy,exit > ρy, lowering π^R for every t. With δ_i = 1 if student i is exposed to the exit exam, their estimating eq. (5) is

  ŵ_it = (r_t·R_{s_i} + a_t·τ_i) + δ_i·(β^r_t·R_{s_i} + β^a_t·τ_i) + e_it,

where β^r_t = r^exit_t − r_t and β^a_t = a^exit_t − a_t. **Proposition 2 / their finding: β^r_t < 0 and β^a_t > 0** — the new individual signal *reduces the return to institutional reputation R* and *raises the return to the individual admission score τ*. Identification comes from the *staggered, field-specific* introduction of graduation exams (variation in δ_i), a difference-in-differences design we mirror conceptually for cross-field variation: fields with stronger individual signaling should show compressed reputation returns.

**(3) The earnings-growth specification (their eq. 6) and dataset.** w_it = d_{cit} + r_0·R_{s_i} + r·(R_{s_i}×t) + a_0·τ_i + a·(τ_i×t) + e_it, with cohort×experience cells d_{cit}; reputation R is the college-mean ICFES percentile (computed over 136 colleges; scores scaled /10 to a 0–10 range), τ is the individual ICFES admission percentile, t = potential experience. Their headline result: **reputation, unlike years of schooling, is positively correlated with earnings *growth* (r > 0)**, consistent with reputation carrying value-added (v_1 > 0), not just sorting.

**Mapping to our project:** selectivity/admission composition → α (student-body ability); education-quality/value-added → v_1 (reputation premium); AR (SpringRank prestige) plays the role of R_s; ER (Scorecard/SDR wages) plays the role of ŵ_it. A large Gap_field corresponds to a regime where employers still load heavily on R (high π^R, weak ρy individual signals = slow learning); the CS near-zero gap corresponds to high-ρy fields where the exam-rollout logic has already "happened" via portfolios/certifications, driving β^r < 0 to its limit. Credential fields (law, finance) are the opposite — reputation stays priced, π^R persists, gap is high.
