# yi2017transferability
**Full citation:** Yi, M., Mueller, S., & Stegmaier, J. (2017). Transferability of Skills across Sectors and Heterogeneous Displacement Costs. American Economic Review: Papers & Proceedings, 107(5), 332-336.
**DOI / URL:** 10.1257/aer.p20171024 (https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.p20171024)
**Access level:** abstract only (AER P&P landing page; verified citation, abstract, methodology, and JEL codes)

## What it contributes to THIS project
Yi, Mueller and Stegmaier give us the sector-level framing that our AR-ER gap inherits: skill transferability *between sectors* (here manufacturing vs. other sectors; for us academia vs. industry) is heterogeneous, and that heterogeneity is what generates heterogeneous displacement/mobility costs. This is the conceptual bridge from Poletaev-Robinson's within-labor-market skill distance to a cross-sector distance, which is precisely the cut our project makes between the academic faculty-hiring sector and the industry labor market. Their central message — the value of a worker's human capital depends on which sector it is redeployed to, and this varies a lot — predicts that the academia-to-industry gap should vary by field exactly as their transferability measure varies by destination sector. We borrow their result as evidence that "task distance to industry" is a real, estimable, and heterogeneous object, supporting our hypothesis that low-distance fields (CS) show near-zero gap while high-distance credential fields (law, finance) show large gaps. It also flags an identification hazard we must address: workers/PhDs sort endogenously into the sector where their skills transfer best.

## Specific equation / result / dataset we reuse
We reuse two things. (1) The *transferability measure* concept — a sector-pair-specific index of how much of a worker's human-capital value survives reallocation to a given destination sector — which we operationalize at the field level as our academia->industry task-distance/transferability score. (2) Their *identification design for endogenous mobility*: a selection-correction (Heckman-type) model paired with a novel instrument built from the worker's social network, used to correct for the fact that observed movers are a selected sample. We adopt the selection-correction logic when relating SDR PhD sector choice (academia vs. industry) and earnings to field-level task distance, since PhDs who leave academia are selected on transferability; their social-network instrument is the template for finding an exclusion restriction for that sector-choice selection.
