# eeckhout2011identifying
**Full citation:** Eeckhout, Jan, & Kircher, Philipp (2011). Identifying Sorting—In Theory. Review of Economic Studies, 78(3), 872-906.
**DOI / URL:** 10.1093/restud/rdq034 (https://academic.oup.com/restud/article-abstract/78/3/872/1569928)
**Access level:** abstract only (read the abstract/result summary via the journal listing, Philipp Kircher's author page, and search snippets; full text paywalled)

## What it contributes to THIS project
This is the key methodological warning for our identification strategy and the most load-bearing of the matching-theory cluster. Eeckhout-Kircher prove that wage data alone *cannot* identify the *sign* of sorting (whether high workers match high or low firms), because in a sorting model the wage a given worker earns is *non-monotonic* in the employer's type — so the standard Abowd-Kramarz-Margolis two-sided fixed effects are mis-specified for recovering sorting. This directly disciplines how we read ER: a behavioral earnings ranking (Scorecard / SDR salaries) is *not* a clean read of employer-side prestige, so we cannot simply equate "high ER" with "high firm type" the way naive AKM would. It pushes us to define ER as observed labor-market outcomes (earnings, sector) and the gap as a rank discordance, while being explicit that the *magnitude* of mismatch — not its sign — is what is identified.

## Specific equation / result / dataset we reuse
We reuse two results: (1) the *non-identification of the sorting sign* from wages, and (2) the positive result that the *strength* of sorting — the magnitude of the production cross-partial and the associated output loss from mismatch — *is* identifiable from wage-and-employment data. This justifies framing Gap_field as a measure of the *magnitude* of discordance between prestige rank and ER rank (a strength-of-mismatch quantity, well-identified) rather than a directional claim, and it is our explicit citation for why we do not lean on AKM-style firm fixed effects to construct the employer-reputation ranking.
