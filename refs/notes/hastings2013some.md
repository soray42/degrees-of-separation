# hastings2013some
**Full citation:** Hastings, Justine S., Christopher A. Neilson, and Seth D. Zimmerman (2013). Are Some Degrees Worth More than Others? Evidence from College Admission Cutoffs in Chile. NBER Working Paper No. 19241 (July 2013, rev. September 2014).
**DOI / URL:** 10.3386/w19241 (https://www.nber.org/papers/w19241)
**Access level:** full text (read the NBER w19241 PDF abstract and front matter)

## What it contributes to THIS project
This paper is the strongest evidence that returns to higher education are heterogeneous BY FIELD, which is the very axis our project organizes around (Gap_field computed within field). Using score-based admission cutoffs at more than 1,100 degree programs, it shows that admission raises long-run earnings a lot for some fields (and for highly selective programs) and little for others — directly motivating why an institution's labor-market reputation must be measured field-by-field, not as a single school ranking. This field heterogeneity is what makes our central hypothesis testable: if returns and reputation diverge differently across fields, then a field-level Gap exists and can be predicted by academia-vs-industry task distance. It also gives us a large-N, long-horizon (up to 30 years) precedent that earnings outcomes linked to administrative records are the right behavioral object for ER.

## Specific equation / result / dataset we reuse
We reuse the design: a separate regression-discontinuity in admission score at each degree-program cutoff, with admissions records linked to administrative earnings records (tax data) up to 30 years post-application, then a mapping from threshold-crossing RD estimates to causal returns-by-degree. The concrete result we lift is the field pattern — "large, positive returns to highly selective degrees and degrees in health, science, and social science fields," with returns to selectivity not varying by socioeconomic status — which we use to motivate and benchmark the field-level dispersion of ER. The per-program RD-at-cutoff and the field-stratified return estimates are the transplantable elements; the join to our work is at the field (CIP) level.
