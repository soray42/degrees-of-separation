\title{Degrees of separation: where the academy's hiring order and graduates' pay agree}
\author{Sora Yng}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Universities sell two products under one name: doctoral graduates, whom the academy hires as faculty, and
bachelor's graduates, whom employers hire. Faculty hiring reveals an ordering of departments, and graduates' pay an
ordering of programs. We ask where these two orderings of the same institutions agree, field by field. Using public
data only, we measure within-field coupling, the rank correlation across institutions between a department's
position in the US faculty-hiring hierarchy and its graduates' median earnings, for 52 US fields (College
Scorecard, with Census PSEO as an overlapping second source) and, with a UK hiring hierarchy built from
ORCID-derived records, for 33 UK subjects (DfE LEO). A short model states when hiring reveals the academy's
valuation of a department and pay the labour market's valuation of its graduates, and what coupling then
measures. The analysis is descriptive. Where the two orderings agree, they agree at the institution's name,
mostly in what both share with the selectivity of its intake: controls for selectivity, Pell share, control and
state lower mean US coupling from $+0.43$ to $+0.14$. About a fifth of pay coupling runs between employer sectors
rather than within them. Below the name they agree little. Within institutions, one standard deviation of department prestige goes
with about 0.8\% higher four-year pay (up to about 3\% corrected for error in the ranks), a remainder whose 95\%
interval reaches zero, individually clear only in computer science; field prestige does not detectably out-predict
academia-wide prestige. In the UK most of the ordering survives a direct control for graduates' prior attainment.
Within fixed graduation cohorts coupling rises by about 0.03 per year in both countries; in the US about three
fifths of the rise is shared with selectivity. Coupling is weak where pay follows national scales. The hiring
ranks partly reflect doctoral production (an audit bounds that part from above), and the data do not say why
agreement below the name is small.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} faculty hiring networks; academic prestige; graduate earnings; college
selectivity; reputation; field of study; administrative data; science of science

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Each faculty hire records where the new professor's doctorate came from. Aggregated over a decade, the net
direction of these hires orders departments in a steep hierarchy in which a few institutions train most of the
faculty in their field \citep{clauset2015systematic, wapman2022quantifying}: a net-placement position. The
ordering comes from peers' hiring decisions, not from admissions statistics, reputation surveys or composite
rankings, and it exists field by field. What employers pay the same institutions' bachelor's graduates orders
them a second time. A university thus sells two products under one name, doctoral graduates to the academy and bachelor's
graduates to the labour market, and each audience's choices reveal an ordering. We ask where the two orderings
agree: within a field, do graduates of departments higher in the hiring hierarchy earn more, and does the
agreement belong to the department or to the institution around it?

The question matters for two literatures. For the science of science, the hiring hierarchy is a carefully
measured status ordering whose positions shape productivity, the spread of ideas and who becomes faculty
\citep{way2019productivity, morgan2018prestige, morgan2022socioeconomic, zhang2022labor}; whether the market's
ordering of the same institutions agrees with it, outside academia, where nearly all bachelor's graduates work, has
not, to our knowledge, been measured.
For the economics and sociology of higher education, it sharpens an old question. Earnings rise with
institutional selectivity and brand, much of that gradient is attributed to who enrols
\citep{dale2002estimating, dale2014estimating, mountjoy2021returns}, the gradient differs by field of study
\citep{eide2016where, quadlin2021same, hastings2013some, bloem2024understanding}, and in the United Kingdom an
institution's academic standing adds little information about graduates' returns beyond the selectivity of its
intake \citep{britton2022how}. A department-level, peer-defined prestige axis asks how much of the status gradient
in pay belongs to the department, and how the gradient changes over careers, the subject of employer-learning
models \citep{farber1996learning, altonji2001employer}.

We measure within-field coupling: the Spearman rank correlation, across the institutions that teach a field,
between a department's position in the US faculty-hiring hierarchy \citep{wapman2022quantifying} and the median
earnings of its bachelor's graduates. We map it across 52 US fields on College Scorecard earnings
\citep{scorecard}, repeat it on Census Post-Secondary Employment Outcomes (PSEO) \citep{pseo, pseo2026}, and, for
the United Kingdom, build an academia-wide hiring hierarchy from ORCID-derived records of PhD-to-faculty moves
\citep{li2026orcid} and set it against Longitudinal Education Outcomes (LEO) earnings, which are also released by
graduates' prior attainment \citep{dfe_leo_provider}. All inputs are public, and every result is descriptive.

A short model (Section~\ref{sec:theory}) reads faculty hiring as revealing a department's net-placement
position, which is the academy's valuation only if hires flow down a single valuation and production-to-hiring
volume does not set their direction, and program pay as the labour market's valuation of a program's graduates,
selection included. Coupling
then measures how far the two orderings agree, in a name, a department and a growth term. The model was written
after most results were known; its only content not fitted to results already seen is nine tests, specified before
the analysis but not publicly registered.

The findings come in three layers. \emph{Relational} findings need only classical measurement error: the hiring
order and the pay order of the same institutions agree to a degree that varies across fields, with a similar field
ordering on the two US earnings sources; the agreement sits mostly at the name, in what field prestige shares with
academia-wide prestige and with institutional selectivity; in the UK most of it survives a direct control for
graduates' prior attainment; it rises with years since graduation within fixed cohorts in both countries (in the US
its career-time part can only be bounded); and it is weak in UK subjects paid on national scales.
\emph{Construct} findings add the model: where the academy's and the market's orderings agree, they agree at the
institution's name, mostly in what both share with the selectivity of its intake; below the name they agree little,
clearly only in computer science. Controls for selectivity, Pell share, control and state lower mean US coupling
from $+0.43$ to $+0.14$, and about a fifth of pay coupling runs between employer sectors, which counts as the
market's valuation only if employers ration access to sectors; within institutions one within-field standard
deviation (SD) of department prestige goes with about 0.8\% higher pay, an interval that reaches zero (up to about
3\% corrected for error in the ranks); and a field's own ordering does not detectably out-predict the academia-wide
ordering. \emph{Mechanism} claims are not made: why agreement below the name is small is left open.

Much of this is known in kind (Section~\ref{sec:background}). What is new is (i) the faculty-hiring hierarchy as
the prestige axis, which separates a department's standing in its field (field prestige, $F$) from its
institution's standing in the academy as a whole (academia-wide prestige, $G$), and with it a within-institution
test of whether higher-standing departments inside the same institution have better-paid graduates; (ii) a model that reads both indicators as revealed valuations of two audiences and names the assumptions
that can fail; (iii) fixed-cohort estimates, in two countries, of how coupling changes with years
since graduation, new in design and scope rather than in direction \citep{thomas2005postbaccalaureate,
macleod2017big}; and (iv) measurement: a reliability bracket and a production audit for the published ranks, a check against
noise in the program medians, and a UK academia-wide hiring hierarchy. The US field map is a replication in kind: its ordering is close to the known ordering of fields
by how steeply pay follows selectivity \citep{bloem2024understanding}.

For the academy's departmental hierarchy the answer is mostly negative. The small remainder beyond institutional
status cannot be attributed to department standing rather than program-level selection, and the computer-science
estimate describes a sample without the University of California campuses and several flagship public departments
(Section~\ref{sec:status}). Further methods and all robustness detail are in the Supplementary Information (SI).

\section{Background and positioning}\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{College quality and selection.} Once application behaviour absorbs selection, the average earnings
payoff to a more selective college is small, with larger returns for disadvantaged students
\citep{dale2002estimating, dale2014estimating}; other designs find sizeable effects in some settings
\citep{hoekstra2009effect, brewer1999does, witteveen2017earnings}. Single quality proxies are noisy \citep{black2006estimating}, estimates depend on common support, the part of
the distribution and the selection controls \citep{black2004robust, andrews2016quantile, cunha2014measuring}, and
the payoff to elite colleges concentrates in the upper tail \citep{zimmerman2019elite, chetty2023diversifying}. Most of this work measures standing by selectivity, rankings,
brand or value added \citep{riehl2019learning, mountjoy2021returns}. The closest academic measure is
\citet{hussain2009university}: in UK graduate surveys an institution's research rating predicts wages but
correlates 0.87 with entry tariff and turns negative, imprecisely, alongside other quality measures.

\paragraph{Field of study and status by field.} Returns to field are large and heterogeneous
\citep{kirkeboen2016field, altonji2012heterogeneity, altonji2016analysis, webber2014lifetime, kim2015field,
andrews2024returns}, and so is the status gradient within field \citep{eide2016where, quadlin2021same,
hastings2013some}. Closest to us, \citet{bloem2024understanding} show on an earlier Scorecard release that median pay barely varies with admit rate in nursing, teacher education
and social work but strongly in computer science, finance and economics; cross-classified models find
selectivity matters more outside STEM \citep{muse2024college}. UK returns by institution and subject were
estimated on surveys \citep{walker2011differences, walker2018university} and now on administrative LEO records
\citep{belfield2018impact, britton2019improving, britton2020impact}. The closest design to ours is
\citet{britton2022how}: within subject, returns at age 30 track the prior attainment of a degree's intake closely
in law, business and economics and not at all in medicine or education, which the authors attribute to centrally
regulated wages, and net of selectivity no subject's league-table rating correlates positively and significantly
with returns. Our field map and UK check, which uses a coarser, band-level attainment control, are replications in
kind.

\paragraph{Faculty-hiring hierarchies.} Centrality in PhD-exchange networks tracks departments' reputational
prestige \citep{burris2004academic}; hiring networks are steeply hierarchical \citep{clauset2015systematic,
wapman2022quantifying}; PhD origin is tightly linked to placement \citep{fowler2007social, headworth2016credential};
placement records have been used to rank departments \citep{amir2008ranking}; and networks divide into clusters with
little hiring between them \citep{tervio2011divisions}. This literature measures
standing within academia; to our knowledge it has not asked whether position in the hierarchy predicts the pay of
the academy's bachelor's graduates, a question made less than obvious by the weak link between research and
teaching quality \citep{hattie1996relationship}.

\paragraph{Employer learning and career dynamics.} In employer-learning models, the wage coefficient on a
credential that employers see at hiring need not change with experience when a hard-to-observe correlate of
productivity is left out \citep{farber1996learning}; with that correlate in the model, the credential's coefficient
falls with experience while the correlate's rises \citep{altonji2001employer}. Employers learn quickly on average
\citep{lange2007speed}, but the speed varies by occupation \citep{mansour2012does}, and learning about a
productivity that keeps changing stays imperfect over whole careers \citep{kahn2014employer}; for college
graduates the loading on test scores is already high at entry and grows little \citep{arcidiacono2010beyond}. Among
students who applied to and were admitted by similar colleges, the premium to a more selective one is small over
the career \citep{dale2014estimating} or fades within a few years \citep{mountjoy2021returns}; prestige premia fade
with experience in Chile \citep{bordon2020employer} and matter less for later promotions in Japan
\citep{araki2016university}, while US graduates of higher-quality colleges have faster early earnings growth
\citep{thomas2005postbaccalaureate} and college reputation correlates with earnings growth in Colombia
\citep{macleod2017big}. Entry conditions and field-specific forces shape careers too \citep{oreopoulos2012short,
roksa2010what, altonji2016cashier, deming2020earnings}; separating career time from calendar time and cohort
requires assumptions \citep{fosse2019analyzing, fosse2019bounding}, and administrative earnings records lose
graduates selectively \citep{foote2026attrition}.

\section{Two audiences, one name: what the indicators and coupling measure}\label{sec:theory}

Why should faculty hiring measure the academy's standing of a department, and program pay the labour market's
valuation of its graduates? This section states when each reading holds and what $\rho_f$ then measures. We wrote
the model after most results of Section~\ref{sec:results} were known; it organises them, and they are not evidence
for it. Its only content not fitted to results already seen is nine tests (T0--T8), specified before the
analysis but not publicly registered, on data that overlap those of results already seen. Derivations, a mapping of 28 results to the
propositions, and each test's specification and result are in \SItheory.

\paragraph{Two audiences, one name.} A department in field $f$ sells doctoral graduates to the field's hiring committees (the academy) and bachelor's
graduates to employers (the market), both under its institution's name. Following \citet{macleod2015reputation} and \citet{tadelis1999name}, a unit's
reputation with an audience is that audience's expectation of the quality of what it buys from the unit. Beliefs are unobserved, but repeated costly choices reveal the orderings they imply: the audience's valuation. Write the academy's valuation of department $(i,f)$ as $s_{if}=\alpha_fa_i+r_{if}$ and the mean
productivity of its bachelor's graduates, selection included, as $\theta_{if}=\mu_f+\beta_fx_i+y_{if}$, with
institution components $a_i$ and $x_i$, departmental deviations $r_{if}$ and $y_{if}$, and $\beta_f$ the reward of
field $f$'s labour market for the institution component. The two sides correlate within a level ($\mathrm{Corr}(a,x)=\chi$, $\mathrm{Corr}(r,y)=\kappa_f$) but, we
assume, not across levels ($\mathrm{Cov}(r,x)=0$, which fails if departments are relatively strong at selective
institutions).

\paragraph{Proposition 1a: what hiring reveals.} Let $A_{uv}$ count field-$f$ faculty at $v$ with a PhD from $u$.
Under vertical sorting (H1), in every pair of departments that exchanges faculty more hires are expected to flow
from the higher-valued to the lower-valued department, and the ordering that minimises upward hires then agrees
with the academy's valuation on every exchanging pair. But H1 holds even without valuation: under random matching,
flows scale with production and hiring, and departments are ordered by their ratio of PhDs produced to faculty
hired. Volume neutrality (H0), that this ratio does not set the direction of flows, is what makes the direction
informative. The published $F$ is SpringRank \citep{debacco2018physical}, which has no degree correction and ranks
net exporters higher at equal valuation. $F$ is therefore a department's net-placement position. It reads as the
academy's valuation, academic prestige or status in a deference network
\citep{burris2004academic,podolny1993status}, only under H0 and H1, and it may partly reinforce itself
\citep{long1979entrance}. Test T0 audits H0 and finds that $F$ partly reflects production volume; test T1 asks
whether one index orders departments both as producers and as employers of PhDs and finds that it does not, which
bears on H1 (Section~\ref{sec:prestige}). We therefore do not claim the valuation reading: $F$ is described as a
net-placement position, not as one belief about the quality of a department's PhDs.

\paragraph{Proposition 1b: what pay reveals.} Suppose that employers compete for graduates and pay their
posterior expectation of productivity, see institution and major at hiring and set pay freely rather than by a
schedule, and that program medians cover the graduates who work for them. An employer's weight on a unit's own
record, $\omega_u=n_u\tau_u^2/(n_u\tau_u^2+\sigma^2)$, grows with the number $n_u$ of the unit's graduates it has
observed and attributed to it ($\tau_u^2$ and $\sigma^2$ are the variances of unit means and of individual
performance). A name pools the graduates of all of an institution's programs, so $\omega_{\text{name}}$ is near one
wherever employers see an institution's graduates often; a department's record counts only where employers
attribute performance to departments. With the name's mean known, the
program's log median $t$ years after graduation is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:theory-wage}
m_{if}(t)=\mu_f(t)+\beta_fx_i+\Lambda_{ft}\,y_{if}+g_{i}(t),\qquad \Lambda_{ft}=\omega_f+(1-\omega_f)\lambda_t ,
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_t=b+(1-b)\,t/(t+k)$ is the weight on a graduate's own revealed performance ($b$ at hiring, $k$ the
speed of learning) and $g_i(t)$ is growth that differs across institutions. Program pay is then the labour
market's valuation of a program's graduates. At the name, employers' belief and the realised mean productivity of the institution's graduates coincide,
selection included. We reserve \emph{employer reputation} for what the
design of \citet{macleod2017big} identifies, a school-mean premium with graduates' own attainment held fixed,
which only the UK attainment bands identify, and coarsely (Section~\ref{sec:uk}). Three further limits apply: raw
pay contains local price levels and access to high-wage labour markets; placement into better-paid sectors counts
as the market's valuation only if employers, not graduates' preferences, allocate it; and where pay follows a
schedule the wage stops revealing valuation (Proposition~5).

\paragraph{Proposition 2: what coupling measures.} With $F=s+e_F$, classical errors and no covariance across
levels,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:theory-decomp}
\mathrm{Cov}\{F_{if},m_{if}(t)\}=\underbrace{\alpha_f\beta_f\,\chi\,\sigma_a\sigma_x}_{\text{name}}
+\underbrace{\Lambda_{ft}\,\kappa_f\,\sigma_r\sigma_y}_{\text{department}}
+\underbrace{\alpha_f\,\mathrm{Cov}\{a_i,g_{i}(t)\}}_{\text{growth}} .
\end{equation}
$G$ pools the hires of all fields, so it carries only the name and growth terms. For Spearman correlations on the
same institutions, $c_F=r_{FG}\,c_G+\sqrt{1-r_{FG}^2}\;\mathrm{sr}_F$ is an identity, where $c_X$ is the coupling
of $X$ with pay, $r_{FG}$ the correlation of $F$ with $G$ and $\mathrm{sr}_F$ the semi-partial correlation of pay
with the part of $F$ orthogonal to $G$. $F$ out-predicts $G$ only if $\mathrm{sr}_F>c_G\tan(\vartheta/2)$, where
$\cos\vartheta=r_{FG}$; at the across-field means ($r_{FG}=0.79$, $c_G=0.45$) the threshold is 0.16 and
$\mathrm{sr}_F$ is 0.08, a gap the model accommodates but does not imply.
Three readings follow. First, coupling measures agreement between two audiences' orderings, not agreement on what
they value: an academy that values research and a market that values graduates' ability agree whenever the two
covary across institutions ($\chi>0$), a primitive, not a result. Second, if one institution factor dominates,
each field's coupling is proportional to its $\beta_f$, and the field map shows how steeply each field's pay
follows the institution factor that status indices share, whatever status index is used; the construct reading of
$F$ then matters only below the name. Test T5, which replaces $F$ by faculty salaries, spending or endowment, is inconclusive. Third, average SAT and the admission rate are noisy measures of intake ability, part of $x_i$;
for the question whether the academy's standing carries information that the market prices beyond intake, they
are confounds.

\paragraph{Proposition 3: below the name.} At entry, if employers see nothing of a graduate beyond the credential
($b=0$), the department term is $\omega_f\kappa_f\sigma_r\sigma_y$: it needs both that department standing carry
information about graduates' productivity ($\kappa_f\neq0$) and that employers attribute performance to
departments ($\omega_f>0$). After entry it needs only $\kappa_f\neq0$, because each graduate's own record prices
the department deviation ($\Lambda_{ft}\ge\lambda_t$). A small department term fits two readings: department
standing carries little information about graduates' market productivity (content), or employers cannot see
departments and learn slowly about individuals (information). If the learning speeds estimated in other
populations \citep{lange2007speed,arcidiacono2010beyond} applied here ($k$ near 3 gives $\lambda_4\approx0.57$), a
small four-year remainder would imply little departmental content; nothing in these data tests this, no test here
separates the two readings, and we make no claim about the mechanism.

\paragraph{Proposition 4: experience.} (a) If employers' posterior mean for a name equals the name's mean
($\omega_i=1$), learning about individuals cannot move program-mean pay, the result of \citet{farber1996learning}
applied to program means. More generally, the loading of pay on a status measure net of selectivity falls with
experience, the signature of \citet{altonji2001employer}, only if beliefs about names are imprecise ($\omega_i<1$)
and employers' entry prior has a status component that the measure tracks beyond selectivity. A name's record
pools many graduates (\SItheory), so under rational expectations $\omega_i$ is near one wherever employers see an
institution's graduates often, and there the absence of a decline is close to guaranteed; it says nothing about
learning about individuals.
(b) A rise in the institution-level covariance of pay with status requires status-correlated growth, such as
access to firms with internal ladders \citep{gibbons1999theory,rivera2015pedigree,weinstein2018employer};
learning about rarely seen names from a prior that under-weights status; migration toward high-wage labour
markets; or a change in who is observed (on standardised scales, also shrinking pay variance unrelated to
status). The shape of the rise does not separate learning from growth; where it occurs could (test T3,
Section~\ref{sec:career}).

\paragraph{Proposition 5: pay schedules.} If a share $\bar\pi_f$ of a field's graduates is paid on a schedule
whose level where they work is $\bar\tau$, then to first order
$\mathrm{Cov}(F,m)=(1-\bar\pi_f)\,\mathrm{Cov}_{\text{market}}(F,m)+\bar\pi_f\,\mathrm{Cov}(F,\bar\tau)$, plus a
term for sorting out of scheduled jobs. Under a national schedule the second term is zero at entry and can grow
over careers only if promotion depends on status; exit that depends on status moves the sorting term. Weak
coupling there is not evidence that the market's ordering disagrees with the academy's; the model's content is a
prediction about quantities, such as where graduates are hired (test T6, Section~\ref{sec:boundary}).

\paragraph{What the model licenses.} Claims come in three layers. \emph{Relational} claims need only classical
measurement error: the hiring order and the pay order of the same institutions agree to a degree that varies across
fields, with a similar field ordering on the two US earnings sources; the agreement sits mostly at the name, in
what field prestige shares with academia-wide prestige and with institutional selectivity; in the UK most of it
survives a direct control for graduates' prior attainment; it rises with years since graduation within fixed
cohorts in both countries (in the US its career-time part can only be bounded); and it is weak in UK subjects paid
on national scales. \emph{Construct} claims add Proposition~1: where the academy's and the market's orderings
agree, they agree at the institution's name, mostly in what both share with the selectivity of its intake; below
the name they agree little, clearly only in computer science (about 0.8\% of pay per SD of department prestige, up
to about 3\% under the lower-bound reliability of $F$). These claims read $F$ as a net-placement position that is
partly production volume, and pay as a valuation that includes selection. Pay is not netted of destination
location: five-year coupling on the PSEO panel is unchanged when the wage level of graduates' destination census
divisions is netted out (ratio $1.01$ \ci{0.88}{1.15}; T7), but the Scorecard estimates (the control ladder, the
partial couplings and the within-institution slope) were not re-estimated net of destination location, location
within divisions is not netted, and a fifth to a third of the career rise moves with destination wages. About a
fifth of pay coupling runs between employer sectors ($0.21$ \ci{0.08}{0.34}; T8), which counts as the market's
valuation only if employers ration access to sectors. The claim below the name also assumes no covariance across
levels, which the within-institution design relaxes only as far as SAT, admission rate, Pell share and $G$ span the
institution component $x_i$. \emph{Mechanism} claims are not made: nothing here says what attending an institution
or department does to pay, that wages equal productivity, that employers read academic rankings (the name term can
be a pure common cause), or why agreement below the name is small. In the mapping of \SItheory{} no result follows
for every admissible parameter value; half (14 of 28) are accommodated by parameter values inferred from the same
results, and three are in tension with the model: one year after graduation field prestige carries the
association and $G$ does not; US special education and UK allied health couple strongly despite pay schedules; and
across PSEO Flows cells the prestige and $G$ gradients of pay are not robustly flatter where more graduates enter
education, health care and public administration.

\subsection{Prestige: the faculty-hiring hierarchy}\label{sec:prestige}

We use the published prestige ranks of \citet{wapman2022quantifying}, estimated with SpringRank from a census of
faculty at 368 PhD-granting US institutions, 2011--2020 \citep{wapman2022data}. For institution $i$ and field
$f$, field (department) prestige $F_{if}$ is minus the published field rank, and academia-wide prestige $G_i$ is
minus the rank in the network that pools hires across all fields. Both are fixed across earnings horizons and
describe hires of 2011--2020. $G$ tracks selectivity: across the 242 universities of the Opportunity Insights
sample its Spearman correlation with average SAT is $+0.68$, and the UK analogue correlates $+0.71$ with intake
selectivity across 133 providers. Within the field-by-cohort cells of the PSEO panel $F$ and $G$ correlate 0.83 on
average.

We keep the labels field and academia-wide prestige, but two tests of Section~\ref{sec:theory} qualify what $F$
measures. $F$ is partly production volume (T0): replacing it by a degree-corrected Bradley--Terry rank keeps
$R_{\mathrm{DC}}=0.84$ \ci{0.74}{0.94} of mean coupling, and residualising it on log research doctorates and the net export ratio of hires keeps $R_{\perp}=0.60$ \ci{0.47}{0.73} (56 fields). The reading fixed in advance is ``partly production''.
Because the residualisation also removes net export that valuation itself produces, $1-R_{\perp}$ bounds the
production share from above. $F$ is also not one index for both roles (T1). With ranks fitted on one half of the
hires and roles scored on the other, departments' orderings as placers and as hirers of PhDs agree less than
SpringRank's own generative model produces under one index: the ratio of cross-role to within-role agreement is
$0.658$ against a benchmark of $0.828$, a difference of $-0.169$ \ci{-0.229}{-0.115} (33 fields), under a
calibration of the benchmark chosen after the literal one had degenerated. The simpler role-consistency ratio also
fails its threshold set in advance ($0.525$ \ci{0.461}{0.583} against 0.80). We therefore read $F$ as a
net-placement position that is partly production, not as one belief about the quality of a department's PhDs
(\SI{2}, \SItheory).

The public release omits institution pairs linked by a single hire, so a SpringRank rebuilt from public edges
reproduces the published field ranks only partly (median Spearman 0.73; academia-wide 0.908). We therefore use the
published ranks and bracket their sampling reliability: the split-half reliability of
$F$ on public edges averages 0.81 across 57 fields \citep[stepped up with the Spearman--Brown
formula;][]{spearman1910correlation, brown1910some}, a lower bound, and 0.96 when extrapolated to the number of
hires behind the published ranks; that of $G$ is 0.99 (\SI{2}). Where reliability matters, results are reported at
both ends.

\subsection{Earnings and placement}\label{sec:earnings}

\paragraph{US: College Scorecard.} The canonical outcome is the median earnings of Title IV--aided bachelor's
completers four years after completion, from the Field-of-Study file released in June 2026 \citep{scorecard},
which excludes completers enrolled or not working in the measurement year, aggregated over each field's four-digit
CIP codes through a curated map of 66 fields. Matching to the published ranks by normalised institution name
gives 3,444 institution-by-field cells in the 52 fields with at least 15 matched institutions (median 48, range
15--173), the canonical sample, and 3,500 cells in the 57 fields with at least 8, used for heterogeneity,
reliability and cross-source statistics. Scorecard covers 66--74\% of the ranked institutions in each
academia-wide prestige quartile (SI check, \SI{1}), but the name join loses institutions whose Scorecard names
carry campus qualifiers (Georgia Tech, Purdue and Ohio State among them). Institutional covariates (average SAT,
admission rate, Pell share, control, institution-wide earnings and the state earnings level) come from the ``most
recent'' institution file, which describes recent entering classes; its SAT average is missing for test-blind
institutions (the University of California campuses and Caltech among them), and for three key statistics we
re-date the controls to the graduates' entry years with the historical files. No public US file measures the
entry attainment of a program's own students. One- and five-year medians refer to other cohorts; they serve as
replications and enter averages over the three horizons.

\paragraph{US: PSEO.} PSEO reports unemployment-insurance wage earnings of graduates with earnings above a
full-time minimum-wage threshold, in constant 2023 dollars, one, five and ten years after graduation (y1, y5, y10)
by three-year graduation cohort. Release V4.13.0 (2025Q4) is canonical; the August 2026 release V4.14.1 (2026Q2),
on which the later analyses run, is a re-check. For career time we keep, within each field and cohort (graduates
of 2001--03 to 2010--12), the institutions observed at all three horizons, at least 15 per cell: 106 institutions,
142 field-by-cohort cells and 39 fields on V4.13.0 (116, 145 and 40 on V4.14.1). A window-by-horizon surface adds
later cohorts: 17 cells, from 2001--03 to 2019--21 at y1, to 2016--18 at y5 and to 2010--12 at y10. PSEO partners
are mostly public: only 20 of the 93 top-quartile ranked institutions have a usable five-year cell on V4.13.0 (26
on V4.14.1). PSEO Flows give each institution-by-CIP-2 cell's employed graduates by employer sector (\SI{7}) and by
census division of work (\SI{6}, \SItheory).

\paragraph{UK: LEO and an academia-wide hiring hierarchy.} The DfE provider-level LEO file reports median
earnings by provider, CAH2 subject, graduating cohort and year after graduation (YAG 1, 3, 5), separately by
prior-attainment band, sex and POLAR4 quintile (one characteristic at a time) \citep{dfe_leo_provider}. We use
the cohorts 2013/14 to 2016/17 and the 33 subjects with at least 15 providers. For prestige we run SpringRank on
11,987 PhD-to-faculty moves between institutions in Great Britain in the ORCID-derived academic mobility edges
\citep{li2026orcid, orcid}, matched to providers through the ROR data dump \citep{ror2026data}: 134 providers form
the primary sample (\SI{1}). Russell Group members sit at a mean percentile of 0.86 against 0.43 for other
providers, and the split-half reliability is 0.92. Validity is less certain: in the US, an academia-wide ranking
rebuilt from the ORCID-derived edges agreed with the published ranks at Spearman 0.74 (\SI{2}). Field-tagged UK
networks are too sparse to use, so the UK analysis has no department axis. Intake selectivity is the
institution's share of graduates who entered with at least 360 tariff points.

\paragraph{Other sources.} Table~\ref{tab:data} lists the other sources: Opportunity Insights tail outcomes, the
2023 American Community Survey (licensed and sector shares, and wage levels by state, census division and sector),
research doctorates from the Survey of Earned Doctorates and IPEDS, state unemployment rates, and admission rules
for computer-science majors coded from official university pages.

\subsection{Coupling and inference}\label{sec:coupling}

Coupling is $\rho_f=\mathrm{Spearman}_i(F_{if},Y_{if})$ across the institutions $i$ that teach field $f$, with $Y$
median earnings: a rank statistic that asks whether the prestige and pay orderings coincide, not how large the pay
differences are. Partial couplings correlate the rank residuals of $F$ and $Y$ after regressing both on the ranks
of the controls. The main text rests on three estimands: coupling and partial coupling averaged over fields; the
within-institution slope $\beta$ of log earnings on department prestige (Eq.~\ref{eq:fe}), a pay difference; and
the per-year change of coupling within fixed graduation cohorts. A field is flagged reliable when its earnings
carry cross-institution signal beyond sampling noise, its interval excludes the band that noise alone would
produce under perfect agreement, and it has at least 10 institutions; 20 of the 52 canonical fields pass, and the
flag is a check, not a filter (\SI{3}).

Fields share institutions, so Scorecard means across fields use a joint institution bootstrap, conditional on
the observed fields; PSEO statistics use the two-way (field,
institution) cluster variance \citep{cameron2011robust}, with the conservative crossed field-by-institution
bootstrap beside it; within-institution slopes use an institution cluster bootstrap; UK intervals resample
subjects and then providers; tests clustered by state use the wild cluster bootstrap \citep{cameron2008bootstrap,
webb2023reworking}; field-level moderators are tested by field-label permutation and by resampling whole CIP-2
families. Brackets are 95\% intervals. Where interval types disagree, an association counts as present only if its
interval excludes zero under the most conservative variance reported for it. Four statements fail that rule and
are reported as not established (\SI{9} lists their intervals): the within-institution slope, with 2026 or with
entry-year selectivity controls; the gap between the growth of the $G$ and $F$ loadings; and the UK per-cell
partial of $G$ net of selectivity on all-graduate medians. The canonical specification was fixed after the main
analyses had been run and before drafting; alternatives form a specification curve
\citep{simonsohn2020specification} (\SI{4}). The analyses added later (scripts 65--69 and 72--75) wrote their tests and reading rules into the script before
estimation, a self-reported timing; the nine tests of Section~\ref{sec:theory} (script 75) were specified before the analysis, after most other
results had been seen, and were not publicly registered. \SI{9} lists every hypothesis examined in scripts
52--75 with its outcome. Every number comes from a seeded script or, where marked ``SI check'', from code run on the
repository's tables.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

Each subsection states a relational finding, which needs only classical measurement error, and the reading that
Section~\ref{sec:theory} licenses. Table~\ref{tab:ladder} collects the controls for
institution-level status and Table~\ref{tab:career} the career-time estimates.

\subsection{The two orderings agree, to a degree that varies across fields}\label{sec:map}

In most fields the academy's hiring order and the market's pay order of the same institutions agree. Across the
52 canonical fields mean coupling is $+0.419$ \ci{+0.355}{+0.482}, and 39 fields have coupling more than 1.96
bootstrap standard errors above zero, none below (Fig.~\ref{fig:map}). Coupling varies across fields by more than
institution sampling produces: in a three-level meta-analytic model of Fisher-$z$ coupling on 57 fields
\citep{konstantopoulos2011fixed, cheung2014modeling}, Cochran's $Q=183.2$ on 56 degrees of freedom and $I^2=0.69$
\citep{higgins2002quantifying}. Propagating noise in the program medians, in a secondary analysis not yet independently verified, lowers $I^2$
to 0.51 and keeps the field ordering (\SI{3}). Part of the
remaining variation is a measurement confound: coupling rises with the number of institutions that teach a field
(Spearman 0.43, 57 fields), and a discipline (CIP-2) variance component, detected without adjustment, is borderline
once field size is a covariate (permutation $p$ from 0.028 to 0.046; \SI{3}). The grouping of fields into
disciplines is therefore a descriptive map.

The field ordering is similar on a second earnings source: coupling from PSEO five-year earnings correlates with
Scorecard coupling across fields at $+0.68$ (two-way interval \ci{+0.54}{+0.82}; 57 fields, V4.13.0) and $+0.74$
\ci{+0.61}{+0.87} on V4.14.1. This is robustness to the earnings measure, not an independent replication: both use
the same $F$, and 61\% of the PSEO rows used on V4.14.1 also enter the Scorecard couplings.

Two checks bear on what the pay side measures. Net of the wage level of the census divisions where graduates
work, five-year coupling on the PSEO panel is unchanged (ratio to raw coupling $1.01$ \ci{0.88}{1.15}, 39 fields;
T7); location within divisions is not netted.
Pay also tracks where graduates go: within CIP-2 families in PSEO Flows, family prestige orders the share of
employed graduates in market sectors about as closely as it orders pay ($+0.40$ \ci{+0.32}{+0.47} against $+0.41$
\ci{+0.31}{+0.49}; 1,312 cells; \SI{10}), and about a fifth of the pay coupling runs between employer sectors
($0.21$ \ci{0.08}{0.34}; T8). That part counts as the market's valuation only if employers, not graduates' own choices, allocate access to
sectors, which these data cannot show.

\subsection{Where the orderings agree: at the institution's name}\label{sec:status}

Where the academy's and the market's orderings agree, they agree at the institution's name, mostly in what both
share with the selectivity of its intake; below the name they agree little, clearly only in computer science.
Within institutions one SD of department prestige goes with about 0.8\% of pay, and up to about 3\% under the
lower-bound reliability of $F$, so the department-specific part is diluted by the name, not shown to be absent.
The pay in these statements is Scorecard pay, not netted of destination location, and about a fifth of pay
coupling runs between employer sectors (T8; Section~\ref{sec:map}), which counts as the market's valuation only if
employers ration access to sectors.
The estimand is the information in $F$ about pay net of institution-level status proxies, not a return. Average
SAT is a candidate quality treatment as well as a selection proxy \citep{dale2002estimating}, so the drop measures
what public data cannot separate from institutional status, not what selectivity causes.

\paragraph{The control ladder.} On a common sample of 44 fields, mean coupling falls from $+0.43$ raw to $+0.31$
with Pell share, control and the state earnings level, to $+0.24$ with the admission rate and to $+0.14$ with
average SAT (Fig.~\ref{fig:status}a; Table~\ref{tab:ladder}). On the 46 fields estimable with the broad set (SAT,
admission rate, Pell share, control and state earnings level) the mean partial coupling is $+0.141$
\ci{+0.075}{+0.208}, and 11 fields stay more than 1.96 standard errors above zero. Dating the controls to the falls
in which the earnings cohort entered (2014--15) leaves the broad partial where it was (difference $+0.005$
\ci{-0.031}{+0.041} on the same 2,787 programs, two-way; \SI{4}). Adding $G$ to the broad set leaves $+0.059$
\ci{+0.003}{+0.114} (44 fields), the part of the department ordering that neither selectivity nor the academia-wide
ordering tracks; across ten specifications that net out selectivity and $G$, its interval excludes zero in four.

\paragraph{Within institutions.} Institution fixed effects absorb what is common to an institution's programs, including the institution-wide
parts of selectivity, $G$ and location. The slope of log
four-year earnings on department prestige falls from $+0.084$ (SE 0.006) to $+0.013$ per within-field SD when they
are added (3,416 programs, 198 institutions; Fig.~\ref{fig:status}b). With field-specific slopes on SAT, admission
rate, Pell share and $G$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:fe}) it is $+0.0084$ \ci{+0.0000}{+0.0164} (2,889 programs, 168
institutions, 47 fields; institution cluster bootstrap; \ci{-0.0012}{+0.0180} under the two-way variance): one SD
of department prestige goes with 0.84\% higher four-year pay, with an upper 95\% limit of 1.6--1.8\% and a lower
limit at or below zero. With one- and five-year medians, which describe other cohorts, the slope is about zero, and averaged over the
three horizons its interval includes zero unless programs are weighted by graduates (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}). The point estimate barely moves with selectivity dated to entry years ($+0.0081$ against $+0.0090$ on the same
2,801 programs), cubic field-specific slopes in $G$ or a field-by-state control for local demand, a weak control
that describes the institution's state (\SI{4}). A field-specific control for the program's own Pell share does not lower it either (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}); with
that control the slope is smaller for aided graduates without a Pell grant than for Pell recipients, a hint of
composition within programs that the partial-correlation design does not repeat (\SI{4}).

\paragraph{Department prestige against its competitors.} In per-field rank regressions on 47 fields, the mean
Shapley share of $R^2$ \citep{budescu1993dominance, gromping2007estimators, shorrocks2013decomposition} is 0.076
for $F$, against 0.173 for selectivity, 0.086 for $G$ and 0.201 for institution-wide earnings, which contain the
field's own graduates (Fig.~\ref{fig:status}c). Before any control, $G$ couples with field earnings slightly more
strongly than $F$ (mean $c_F-c_G=-0.049$ \ci{-0.085}{-0.011}, 57 fields), but $F$ is measured with more error. By
the identity of Proposition~2, $F$ beats $G$ only where the part of $F$ orthogonal to $G$ correlates with pay by
more than $c_G\tan(\vartheta/2)$, which happens in 15 of 57 fields. Corrected for reliability
\citep{spearman1904proof}, the difference is $-0.005$ \ci{-0.043}{+0.044} at the lower-bound and $-0.042$
\ci{-0.077}{-0.001} at the extrapolated reliability, and net of selectivity it is $+0.00$ \ci{-0.05}{+0.05}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:status}d). Field prestige thus does not detectably out-predict academia-wide prestige under any
correction, although the upper 95\% limits ($+0.044$ under the lower-bound reliability, $+0.05$ net of
selectivity) leave room for a small advantage; whether it predicts pay worse depends on its reliability.

\paragraph{Where the remainder sits.} Only computer science, one of three fields named in advance as
high-coupling, has an individually clear remainder: $+0.050$ \ci{+0.012}{+0.096} per SD pooled over horizons (128
programs; \ci{+0.007}{+0.114} correcting for the three fields). Whether per-field slopes differ at all depends on
whether each field's earnings gradient in $G$ may be curved, and the concentration in computer science and
engineering was read off the results (\SI{4}). The computer-science sample lacks 21 of the 50 highest-ranked
departments: the six University of California campuses and Caltech, which have no fall-2024 SAT average; 11 lost
in the name join, among them Georgia Tech, Purdue, Ohio State and the University of Washington; and 3 without a
one- or five-year median. Entry-year SAT averages and a join by institution identifier would restore most of them, but neither
re-estimate was run, so the computer-science result describes this sample only. A comparison of programs that restrict entry to the major with programs that students can declare is too weak to
separate selection into the major from department standing (\SI{4}).

\paragraph{Two readings.} The residual prestige measure is noisy, and partialling $G$ removes some genuinely
field-specific variation, so the remainder is biased toward zero: disattenuated with the reliability bracket, the
within-institution slope is $+0.0098$ to $+0.0297$. A coefficient-stability bound \citep{oster2019unobservable}
puts the selection on unobservables needed to erase it at 0.20--0.67 times the selection on the observed controls
($R_{\max}=1$), below the usual benchmark of 1 (\SI{4}). Under one reading, the academy's field-specific hierarchy
carries labour-market information of its own; under the other, coupling is the field's gradient in institutional
status plus student quality that institution-level measures miss, for example selection into majors that admit on
grades \citep{bleemer2022will}. The bulk of the coupling fits the second; for the remainder, including the
Pell/non-Pell contrast, the two predict the same pattern. The cross-field ordering does not separate them either:
partial couplings still rank fields like raw couplings beyond shared estimation noise, but raw coupling and how
steeply each field's earnings follow SAT have a true-score correlation of $+0.94$ (23 fields; \SI{4}), which one
dominant institution factor would produce (Proposition~2). Nor does the upper tail: net of selectivity and parental income, $G$'s association with the share of former
students in the top 1\% does not detectably exceed its association with median earnings (242 universities;
\SI{8}).

\subsection{UK: most coupling survives a direct prior-attainment control}\label{sec:uk}

The UK data add what the US data lack, earnings by graduates' own prior attainment, but they have no department
axis, so they bear on institution-level status against student attainment, not on the department question.

UK coupling of $G$ with LEO earnings five years after graduation averages $+0.486$ \ci{+0.398}{+0.559} across 33
subjects, similar to the US coupling of $G$ with Scorecard earnings ($+0.450$, 57 fields). Most of it survives a
control for attainment (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}, panel C; Fig.~\ref{fig:uk}). Among same-band graduates across
providers coupling is $+0.227$ against $+0.342$ for all graduates of the same providers (28 subjects), so 66\%
\ci{51}{79} survives, and 87\% \ci{74}{100} once the lower reliability of band medians is corrected for (27
subjects). Standardising each provider's earnings for its band mix, with band gradients measured within providers,
leaves 85\% \ci{80}{90} (32 subjects). These shares describe orderings; in pay terms less survives, 73\%
\ci{67}{78} of the log-earnings gradient in $G$. Coarse bands, sorting within a band, and within-provider gradients
identified from students admitted despite a lower band all push the shares up (\SI{5}).

Among graduates of the same attainment band, what survives tracks intake selectivity at least as closely as
hiring-network prestige if $G$ is measured with little error: $G$ adds $+0.033$ beyond selectivity (two-way
\ci{-0.047}{+0.112}), and selectivity keeps $+0.252$ \ci{+0.172}{+0.310} beyond $G$. On all-graduate medians $G$
keeps more: net of selectivity $+0.131$ \ci{+0.078}{+0.188}, 26\% of the raw coupling, a fall comparable to the US
one. The UK $G$ may measure standing with error: at a validity of 0.74 the data allow a same-band
partial up to $+0.26$ (\SI{5}). Subjects differ, and the differences survive the attainment control, but subjects
whose pay couples strongly with $G$ are those whose pay couples strongly with intake selectivity (Spearman $+0.92$
\ci{+0.71}{+0.95}); as in the US, the subject ordering cannot be separated from subject-specific selectivity
gradients (\SI{5}).

The within-band premium to intake selectivity net of $G$ is the closest these data come to employer reputation
in the sense of \citet{macleod2017big}. Within fixed cohorts and on average over bands it does not decay: its
partial correlation rises by $+0.027$ \ci{+0.016}{+0.037} per year from YAG1 to YAG5 (T4; 27 subjects), or
$+0.0040$ \ci{+0.0027}{+0.0054} log points per SD of selectivity per year, the opposite of what employer learning
predicts if employers do not see attainment. Like the other UK within-cohort slopes, it mixes years since
graduation with calendar time (Section~\ref{sec:career}). We therefore do not describe the premium as reputation
that learning corrects. The rise sits in the middle bands, and the top band has cells only for medicine and
dentistry, whose graduates are paid on national scales, so it says little about the band where own attainment is
most uniform (\SItheory).

\subsection{Agreement grows with years since graduation}\label{sec:career}

Within fixed graduation cohorts, coupling rises with years since graduation in both countries. In the US it also
rises across graduation cohorts, much of the within-cohort rise is shared with selectivity, and in a weakly
identified decomposition it appears in the loading on academia-wide prestige $G$, not on field prestige $F$.

\paragraph{US within cohorts.} On the balanced PSEO panel, mean coupling rises from $+0.173$ one year after
graduation to $+0.310$ at five years and $+0.451$ at ten (39 fields, V4.13.0; Fig.~\ref{fig:career}a). The mean
per-field slope is $+0.031$ per year (two-way interval \ci{+0.021}{+0.041}; $+0.030$ on V4.14.1,
Table~\ref{tab:career}). It is positive in each of the four cohorts and in both segments, so it does not rest on the first-year point,
and a correction for sampling noise in the medians does not shrink it (exploratory; \SI{6}).

\paragraph{Cohorts and calendar time.} A within-cohort change mixes career time with calendar time. On the 17-cell
surface of graduation windows by horizon (24 fields whose institutions are present in every cell; V4.14.1),
coupling rises both within windows ($\theta_h=+0.031$ \ci{+0.019}{+0.043} per year since graduation) and across
windows at a fixed horizon ($\theta_c=+0.015$ \ci{+0.008}{+0.023}; Fig.~\ref{fig:career}b). Career, calendar and cohort trends are not separately identified (Section~\ref{sec:methods}); if calendar and
cohort trends are both non-negative, the career-time slope lies in $[\theta_h-\theta_c,\theta_h]=[+0.015,+0.031]$
per year \citep{fosse2019bounding}, and the calendar-matched contrast gives nearly the same bound
(Table~\ref{tab:career}). The bound also assumes that graduate study does not depress the early medians of
higher-status institutions more than others'; under that confound both ends are biased upward. Prestige is measured once, from 2011--2020 hires, so any drift as cohorts approach that period enters $b$ or $g$;
tests of entry conditions \citep[cf.][]{oreopoulos2012short} are not informative (\SI{6}).

\paragraph{Selection into the data, graduate study and location.} Higher-prestige institutions have fewer
graduates with PSEO earnings at y1; partialling coverage out removes about a fifth of the slope
(Table~\ref{tab:career}; \citealp[cf.][]{foote2026attrition}). Graduate study could raise coupling mechanically if graduates of higher-status institutions more often leave the
earnings sample to study and return with higher pay. With coverage and the institution's research-doctorate rate per
bachelor's graduate as controls, the rise is $+0.0103$ \ci{+0.0027}{+0.0178} per year, against $+0.0305$ raw on the
same 144 cells; but the doctorate rate is institution-wide and tracks $G$ (within-cell Spearman $+0.71$), so the
removed share mixes timing with a status gradient. At the field level the mechanism is neither shown nor excluded
(power 0.31). About one fifth to one third of the rise moves with the wage level of the census divisions where
graduates work and with the out-of-state share (T7). The rise survives every available proxy; its size does not
(\SI{6}).

\paragraph{Selectivity and status loadings.} Net of SAT and admission rate dated to the cohorts' entry years, the
within-cohort slope is $+0.011$ \ci{+0.003}{+0.020} per year, 0.39 of the raw $+0.029$ on the same 109 cells
(V4.14.1): about three fifths of the rise is shared with selectivity. The loading of pay on $G$ net of selectivity
(the broad set, and $F$ in the rank regression) does not decline within
cohorts: its slope is $+0.016$ \ci{-0.011}{+0.044} per year in the rank regression, which excludes a decline
steeper than 30\% of the uncontrolled rise, and $+0.014$ \ci{+0.003}{+0.024} as a partial correlation (37 fields,
V4.14.1). This test takes selectivity from the 2026 file, whose admission rates rank the PSEO institutions only
loosely like those of their entry years (Spearman 0.45--0.47; SAT 0.88--0.90), a dating that favours finding no
decline. In the UK, $G$'s loading net of selectivity is flat ($+0.008$ \ci{-0.006}{+0.022}, two-way) while the
selectivity loading rises ($+0.024$ \ci{+0.011}{+0.037}); their difference includes zero, and with a negative UK
cross-cohort drift these are not career-time statements. By Proposition~4a, the absence of a decline is close to guaranteed under rational expectations about names that
employers see often: it fits precisely known names, or an entry prior whose status component $G$ does not track, and it does
not bear on learning about individuals. The rising UK selectivity loading does not favour precisely known names;
it fits learning about names, selectivity-correlated growth or a common rise of all standardised loadings
(\SI{6}).

\paragraph{Academia-wide against field prestige.} In a standardised rank regression of earnings on $F$ and $G$
within each field-by-cohort cell, $b_F$ stays flat ($+0.189$ at y1, $+0.177$ at y10) while $b_G$ grows from
$-0.013$ to $+0.328$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:career}d). Their slope difference $d_G-d_F$ is $+0.039$ \ci{+0.009}{+0.069}
under the two-way variance (V4.13.0), but the conservative crossed bootstrap reaches zero, and the decomposition is
weakly identified: within cells $F$ and $G$ correlate 0.83, and $G$ is measured more reliably. In a pooled form of
the regression, and net of selectivity, $G$'s lead over $F$ is not distinguishable from zero (\SI{6}). The rise of the $G$ loading is not detectably faster or slower where employers are likely to see an institution's
graduates less often: on V4.14.1 (40 fields), the $G$ gradient rises about as fast at institutions with low as
with high undergraduate enrolment given $G$ ($+0.032$ and $+0.033$ per year; interaction $+0.0002$
\ci{-0.0074}{+0.0078} per year, permutation minimum detectable effect 0.009; T3). What the growing $G$-tracked component is (residual selectivity,
reputation, resources, location or the timing of graduate study) is not identified.

\paragraph{UK.} On fixed LEO cohorts (33 subjects), coupling rises from $+0.379$ at YAG1 to $+0.484$ at YAG5,
$+0.026$ per year \ci{+0.014}{+0.037} (Fig.~\ref{fig:career}c), in all four cohorts and both segments, and by
$+0.028$ \ci{+0.016}{+0.038} after within-provider standardisation for prior attainment. The calendar-matched
contrast ($+0.031$ \ci{+0.021}{+0.042}) exceeds the within-cohort change on the same providers ($+0.025$), so the
sign assumption of the US bound holds only within sampling error, and offsetting calendar and cohort effects
cannot be excluded (\SI{5}).

\paragraph{Reading.} The direction agrees with descriptive evidence \citep{thomas2005postbaccalaureate,
macleod2017big}, and a descriptive gradient can rise while the causal selectivity premium is small or fades
\citep{dale2014estimating, mountjoy2021returns}. Given the three fifths shared with selectivity, the most
parsimonious reading is residual selection, growing pay returns to pre-college differences that status and
selectivity proxy; holding both fixed, institutions with more Pell students lose their early relative position
(\SI{6}). The rise also fits the other sources of Proposition~4b, among them sorting into careers with steeper
earnings profiles \citep{binder2016career, rivera2015pedigree}, migration and the timing of graduate study;
aggregate data cannot separate them.

\subsection{Where agreement is weak: national pay scales and strictly licensed occupations}\label{sec:boundary}

\paragraph{UK.} Nursing and midwifery, medicine and dentistry, and education and teaching, three subjects whose
graduates are largely paid on national scales, couple at $+0.120$ \ci{-0.033}{+0.258} against $+0.523$
\ci{+0.439}{+0.585} for the other 30 subjects (difference $-0.404$ \ci{-0.566}{-0.238}; exact subject-label
permutation $p=0.0009$; Fig.~\ref{fig:boundary}b), and the contrast survives standardisation for prior attainment
(\SI{5}). Allied health, much of it also paid on national bands, is an exception at $+0.570$. The pattern and its
reading as centrally regulated pay are \citeauthor{britton2022how}'s \citeyearpar{britton2022how}; we add nursing
and a hiring-network axis.

\paragraph{US.} Across US fields the prestige--pay association net of geography falls with the share of a field's
graduates in strictly licensed occupations (health practitioners, school teachers, lawyers, architects and
psychologists; ACS 2023): Spearman $-0.53$ to $-0.48$ over two institution samples and three geography codings
(Fig.~\ref{fig:boundary}a). It is a contrast between discipline families (engineering, computer science, statistics and business at the
low-share end), and it is fragile: under resampling of whole CIP-2 families its interval excludes zero in only 3 of
6 versions, it is not detected within families or with a broad licensure share, a government-or-non-profit
employment share predicts it at least as well, and special education, mostly paid on district salary schedules,
couples strongly. Pay set by the setting, such as a teacher
salary schedule \citep{biasi2021labor} or a pay scale, is a reading consistent with these contrasts; whether
licensure itself raises wages is contested \citep{kleiner2013analyzing, redbird2017new}, and these data isolate
neither reading (\SI{7}).

\paragraph{Quantities.} Where pay follows a schedule the wage stops revealing the market's valuation
(Proposition~5), so weak coupling there says little about whether the market's ordering agrees with the
academy's; the model's content is about quantities. In education and in health professions (CIP-2 51; PSEO Flows
do not release nursing separately), scored on teachers' and registered nurses' pay, $G$ orders institutions by the
scheduled pay level of the census divisions where their graduates work five years after graduation (mean Spearman
$+0.238$ \ci{+0.065}{+0.380}; T6, supported), mainly in health professions ($+0.319$ \ci{+0.159}{+0.451}, 116
institutions; education $+0.157$ \ci{-0.058}{+0.330}). The index uses nominal division wages and largely records
the institution's own division, so it does not separate status from location (\SItheory).

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Where the two orderings agree.} The academy's hiring order and the market's pay order of the same
institutions agree in most fields, increasingly so with years since graduation. Where they agree, they agree at
the institution's name, mostly in what both share with the selectivity of its intake; below the name they agree
little, clearly only in computer science, with about 0.8\% of pay per SD of department prestige (up to about 3\%
under the lower-bound reliability of $F$). The pay in these statements is not netted of destination location
beyond a check on five-year coupling (T7), and about a fifth of pay coupling runs between employer sectors (T8),
which counts as the market's valuation only if employers ration access to sectors. Because $G$ pools the same
hiring data over all fields, ``the name'' here is the part of the hierarchy shared across an institution's
departments.

\paragraph{For the science of science.} The faculty-hiring hierarchy is a reliable measure of position inside the
academy, the academia-wide ordering especially, but it is a net-placement position that partly reflects doctoral
production (the audit bounds that share from above), not one index of a department's standing as producer and as
employer of PhDs. Studies that use
hiring-network prestige as a proxy for a program's training quality or labour-market standing should expect it to
behave mostly as a measure of institutional status. The one field-specific exception, computer science, fits selection into the major as well as department
standing.

\paragraph{For the college-quality literature.} A hiring-network axis shows the pattern \citet{britton2022how}
report: most of the prestige ordering of pay goes with selectivity (US $+0.43$ to $+0.14$ across about 50 fields;
UK $+0.50$ to $+0.13$ on 32 subjects), and what remains beyond selectivity and academia-wide status is small.
Unlike their league-table result, 11 of 46 US fields keep a partial coupling more than 1.96 standard errors above
zero net of the broad selectivity set (without $G$). The cross-field ordering of coupling is nearly collinear with how steeply each
field's pay follows institutional selectivity \citep{bloem2024understanding}, as one dominant institution factor
would produce, so at the resolution of public data ``the status gradient in pay differs by field'' and ``the
department-prestige gradient in pay differs by field'' cannot be told apart. In the UK the within-band premium to
intake selectivity does not decay on average over bands within fixed cohorts (it rises), so we do not describe it
as reputation that learning corrects; the top band, where own attainment is most uniform, cannot answer.

\paragraph{Career time.} Within fixed cohorts coupling rises by about $+0.03$ per year in both countries. In the
US its career-time part is bounded, $[+0.015, +0.031]$ per year, only under a sign assumption; about three fifths
of the within-cohort rise is shared with selectivity, and a fifth to a third moves with where graduates work. Net
of selectivity the status loading does not decline in either country, but under rational expectations about names that employers see often that is close to guaranteed, so it is not
evidence about how employers learn about individuals. Set against small or fading causal selectivity premia \citep{dale2014estimating, mountjoy2021returns},
the most parsimonious reading of the rise is residual selection; it also fits status-correlated growth, sorting
into steeper careers and the timing of graduate study, and it is not detectably concentrated where employers
rarely see an institution's graduates. The data do not distinguish these readings.

\paragraph{Below the name.} Why agreement below the name is small is not identified. Department standing may carry
little information about graduates' market productivity, or employers may not attribute performance to
departments and learn slowly about individuals. If learning speeds estimated in other populations
\citep{lange2007speed, arcidiacono2010beyond} applied here, the small four-year remainder would imply little
departmental content; nothing in these data tests this, and no test here separates the two readings.

\paragraph{What would decide the open questions.} Four kinds of data would separate department standing from
program-level selection: program-level entry attainment, which no public US file has; department ratings from
different assessment rounds \citep{nrc1995research, nrc2011data} paired with PSEO cohorts; admission routes into
majors for a larger sample; and linked student records with entry scores, major and earnings, the decisive
design, which are not public. Census-level hiring edges would narrow the reliability bracket of $F$ and allow a
degree-corrected ranking without the single-hire suppression. Two re-estimates are feasible with the public files
already used: the within-institution and computer-science analyses with a join by institution identifier and
entry-year SAT averages, and the status-loading test with entry-year selectivity. Graduate enrolment by
institution, field and cohort would test the graduate-study reading of the career rise.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

Each of the following limits a specific claim. \emph{Theory.} The model was written after most results had been
seen; it organises them, and they are not evidence for it. Its nine tests were specified before the analysis but
not publicly registered, and they use data that overlap those of results already seen (the pay side of T2 was
known, T3 was chosen knowing that the $G$ loading rises, T4 knowing the within-band rise of raw coupling, T5 had a
known selectivity analogue, and T6--T8 reuse PSEO Flows data analysed in script 61); T2 and T5 are inconclusive for
lack of power, and the amended T1 verdict rests on a calibration chosen after the literal one had degenerated
(\SItheory). \emph{Medians and coverage.} Scorecard medians describe Title IV--aided completers who
work and are not enrolled; PSEO lacks most of the top prestige quartile, and neither source sees the upper tail
within a program. \emph{Sample.} The within-institution and computer-science samples lack 18 to 21 of the 50 highest-ranked
computer-science departments (Section~\ref{sec:status}). \emph{Selectivity proxies.} Selectivity is institution-level; re-dating it
to entry years moves none of the three primary statistics detectably, but cannot show that the proxies capture
student quality, and whatever they miss stays in every partial coupling; the status loadings, the $+0.059$
remainder and the $+0.94$ use 2026 controls. \emph{Location.} Pay is not netted of destination location: only
raw five-year coupling on the PSEO panel was checked net of the wage level of graduates' destination census
divisions (unchanged, T7); the Scorecard estimates (the control ladder, the partial couplings and the
within-institution slope) were not re-estimated net of destination location, location within divisions is not
netted, and the local-demand control describes the institution's state. \emph{Earnings
reliability.} The noise propagation is secondary, not yet independently verified, and its error model is
approximate. \emph{Timing.} Career dynamics are not separable from calendar time without a sign assumption, or from
the timing and returns of graduate degrees, which could bias both ends of the bound upward. \emph{Prestige.} The
reliability of the published field ranks can only be bracketed (0.81--0.96), so the size of the remainder is
uncertain ($+0.0098$ to $+0.0297$ disattenuated); $F$ is partly production volume, a share the audit bounds only
from above, since neither of its branches isolates production; and the UK hierarchy rests on
self-reported ORCID careers of uncertain validity. \emph{Rank statistics.} Low coupling in UK pay-scale subjects may reflect little cross-provider pay variation.
\emph{Descriptive design.} Nothing here separates value added, selection and employer screening, and beliefs about
prestige are not measured. \emph{Field-level inference.} Moderators measured once per field rest on 40--50 fields
and about 18 CIP-2 families. \emph{Power.} Nulls are statements of what the data cannot detect; \SI{9} gives the
power or interval of the main nulls. \emph{Specification.} The canonical specification was fixed after the main
analyses had been run; \SI{4} shows how the main statistics move with the alternatives.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\paragraph{Within-institution model.} For the program of field $f$ at institution $i$,
\begin{equation}
\log Y_{if}=\alpha_i+\gamma_f+\beta\,z(F)_{if}+\sum_{f'} D_{f'}\big(d_{f'}\,z\mathrm{SAT}_i+a_{f'}\,z\mathrm{ADM}_i+p_{f'}\,z\mathrm{Pell}_i+t_{f'}\,zG_i\big)+e_{if},\label{eq:fe}
\end{equation}
where $z(\cdot)$ standardises within field, $\alpha_i$ and $\gamma_f$ are institution and field fixed effects, and
$D_{f'}$ indicates field $f'$. The field-specific slopes let the earnings gradients in selectivity, Pell share and
$G$ differ by field, so $\beta$ is identified only from departments whose standing differs from what their
institution's traits imply. Institutions with one field and fields with fewer than 15 programs are dropped
iteratively; intervals come from an institution cluster bootstrap with 999 draws shared across specifications, so
that differences are paired (\SI{4}).

\paragraph{Career-time contrasts.} On institutions observed in all three cells, the within-cohort change
$(\text{y10}-\text{y1})/9$, the calendar-matched contrast (cohort $c$ at y10 against cohort $c+9$ at y1, over 9) and
the cross-cohort drift at y1 equal $a+b$, $a-g$ and $b+g$ in a linear model with career, calendar and cohort effects
$a$, $b$ and $g$; on the 17-cell surface, the within-window and across-window slopes equal $\theta_h=a+b$ and
$\theta_c=b+g$. Only under $b,g\ge0$ is the career-time effect bounded: $a\in[a-g,\,a+b]$, that is, between the
calendar-matched contrast and the within-cohort change, or $a\in[\theta_h-\theta_c,\,\theta_h]$ on the surface
\citep{fosse2019bounding}. No point estimate of $a$ is reported (\SI{6}).

\paragraph{Further details.} The SI documents data (S1), prestige measures and the audits of $F$ (S2), the
coupling estimator (S3), selectivity designs (S4), the UK analysis (S5), career time (S6), licensing and employer
sector (S7), tail outcomes (S8), power and the hypotheses examined (S9), earnings as a placement measure (S10), and
the model, its mapping of results and tests T0--T8 (S11).

\paragraph{Availability of data and materials.} All inputs are public: the faculty-hiring data \citep{wapman2022data}; College Scorecard field-of-study,
institution and historical institution files \citep{scorecard}; Census PSEO earnings and flows, releases V4.13.0
(2025Q4) and V4.14.1 (2026Q2) \citep{pseo, pseo2026}; the Department for Education's ``Graduate outcomes (LEO):
provider level data'', 2022-23 release \citep{dfe_leo_provider}; the ORCID-derived academic mobility edges
\citep[version 20260419, doi:10.5281/zenodo.19651302;][]{li2026orcid}, built from ORCID public data
\citep{orcid}, with institutions resolved through the ROR data dump \citep[v2.8,
doi:10.5281/zenodo.20512981;][]{ror2026data}; Opportunity Insights college tables, including the Barron's 2009
selectivity index \citep{chetty2020income}; the 2023 American Community Survey public-use microdata
\citep{census_acs2023_pums}; the Survey of Earned Doctorates by baccalaureate institution \citep{ncses_sed_bacc}
and IPEDS completions and directory files \citep{nces_ipeds}; state unemployment rates from the Bureau of Labor
Statistics Local Area Unemployment Statistics \citep{fred_laus}; occupational prestige ratings
\citep{hughes2024occupational, condon2022prestige} and the New York Fed college labour-market data by major
\citep{nyfed2026labor}; and official university admissions pages, saved with their access dates. Provenance for
every input (URL, version, access date and licence) is recorded in \texttt{data/raw/SOURCES.md} of the repository.
No input dataset is redistributed; the derived tables are regenerated by the scripts. The hand-coded
computer-science admission file (URL, access date and verbatim sentence for each program), which no script can
regenerate because the pages change, and the small derived tables (UK prestige ranks, CIP map) are available from
the author.

\paragraph{Code availability.} The numbers in this article come from seeded scripts (scripts 01--75; mainly
52--75). The figures, and the numbers marked ``SI check'', come from \texttt{scripts/70\_paper\_figures.py} (with
\texttt{--si-checks}); the SI names the source of every other number. The repository is at
\url{https://github.com/soray42/degrees-of-separation}. At the time of writing, scripts 67--69 and 72--75 and the
current versions of scripts 65 and 70 are not yet in the public repository; they will be added, with the digests of
their pre-specification blocks, in a tagged release. The binding texts of tests T0--T8 are reproduced in the
docstring of script 75 with their SHA-256 digests; no public time-stamped deposit was made before the tests were
run.
